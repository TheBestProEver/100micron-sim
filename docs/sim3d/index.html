Silica or graphite: which dust clumps?

Two tubules side by side in microgravity, one with silica grains and one with graphite grains, both started from exactly the same grains, positions and speeds. Grains that hit slowly stick and from then on move together as one rigid clump; faster ones bounce. Everything here was computed by the python code in this repository (dolle_core), either earlier (the recorded replays) or right now in your browser (the live run).

Why the total momentum stays constant: every impact pushes the two bodies with equal and opposite impulses (Newton's third law), and the code books exactly what the walls receive and what the residual acceleration gives, so the total (grains flying + walls received − acceleration gave) can change only by floating-point rounding, about 10−16 per impact.

One impact up close

Two grains of 50 µm radius meet head-on, seen from their centre of mass (each moves at half the approach speed). At touch the sticky JKR contact snaps on, the grains squash by 2 to 20 nanometres, push apart, and either the neck between them breaks (bounce) or too little energy is left to break it (stick). The speeds are 0.8 and 1.5 times each pair's sticking speed.

About this page

What it shows

The DOLL-E experiment measures the speed below which dust grains of about 100 µm stick when they collide, for carbon and for silicates. For two 50 µm-radius grains the contact model gives a sticking speed of 6.2 mm/s for silica and 38 mm/s for graphite (computed by dolle_core.sticking_threshold; notebook 03 explains the factor of about 6). The recorded replay starts both tubules from one compact cloud moving at about 15 mm/s (ASSUMED), between the two thresholds: in silica most impacts bounce, in graphite they all stick, so graphite forms far more clumps (12 clumps holding 28 grains against silica's 2 holding 4).

Each tubule is DOLL-E's 0.5 × 0.5 × 1.5 inch (12.7 × 12.7 × 38.1 mm) borosilicate tubule; the long axis points up the rocket. Grains that stick to a wall stay there. Every impact is resolved by the same contact physics as the full simulator (JKR adhesion, damping, yield, friction; the materials' values are sourced in materials.py).

What rigid clumps leave out

Once grains stick here, they never roll, slide or twist over each other: a clump is a rigid body, and an impact on it is decided at the single contact point by the same head-on contact model, with friction for the sideways part. A clump can still lose grains (erosion) or shatter when hit hard; the thresholds for that (Dominik & Tielens 1997) were derived for large aggregates and are extrapolated to these small clumps, and the energy the fragments fly apart with is ASSUMED (here all of what the impact dissipated beyond the bonds; giving none back instead turns silica's 4 sticks into 6). Each impact is treated as an instant (a contact lasts about 1 µs). Left out are rolling and restructuring inside clumps (impacts here carry about 3 % (silica) and 0.5 % (graphite) of the energy needed to start rolling, but the rolling resistance is uncertain by about 50 times, so for silica this is not settled), gas drag (speeds change by under 0.1 % in 12 s), the payload's slow roll, contact charging, and the forces the full simulator switches off as negligible. Notebook 06 derives every step and gives the size of each left-out effect and the check that shows it is small.
