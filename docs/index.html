When does space dust stick together?

Planets begin as dust grains that collide and stick. DOLL-E is an experiment that collides tiny carbon and silicate grains in microgravity to find how slowly they must hit to stick. This site simulates the experiment: a scaled-down version runs in your browser, and full-size desktop runs can be replayed.

What the simulation predicts

38 mm/s

Carbon (graphite) grains stick below this speed, with the commonly used material values.

6 mm/s

Silica grains stick below this speed, so carbon sticks at about six times higher speed. How sticky dry silica is in vacuum is debated.

2 cameras

are needed to measure it: with one camera the answer comes out up to 4× too low.

Can I trust it?

Where the physics comes from. Published contact-mechanics and aerosol-physics papers, textbook mechanics and electrostatics, and a few modelling choices of our own. The Sources page lists every part with its paper, where it is in the code, how it is checked, and what it assumes.

How it is checked. 22 tests compare the code with exact solutions, independent calculations and conservation laws. You can run them yourself on the Verify page.

REBOUND. None of the simulator comes from REBOUND. That widely used N-body code is used only as an outside check: it reproduces our collision-free grain trajectories to under a nanometre, and our collision statistics in a simplified chamber. It does not test the sticking physics.

What is uncertain. How sticky and how bouncy the real grains are. The predictions above use commonly used literature values for stickiness (surface energy) and our own estimates for bounciness, and both matter: twice the surface energy moves the predicted sticking speed about 1.8×, and our assumed bounciness sets most of each number. Some published work suggests dry silica in vacuum is about ten times stickier than the value used here, which would make it as sticky as carbon. Measuring this is exactly what the experiment is for.
