# Provenance: where every part of DOLL-E comes from

Generated from `dolle_sim/provenance.py` (do not edit by hand; run `python tools/build_site.py`).

**None of the DOLL-E physics comes from REBOUND.** No REBOUND code is copied, translated or called by the simulator, and it runs with REBOUND unavailable (tested).

REBOUND is used in one optional file, **dolle_sim/rebound_check.py** (417 of 4963 lines). That file re-runs a simplified chamber inside REBOUND as an outside check of our trajectory integration and collision bookkeeping; it does not check the sticking physics. Two of our methods are standard ones REBOUND also uses (second-order leapfrog / velocity Verlet and direct-sum gravity), and our collision detection is related to its 'line' mode, all written independently.

The physics comes from published contact-mechanics and aerosol papers, textbook mechanics and electrostatics, and some derivations and choices of our own. Of the 38 components listed: 8 published, 15 standard, 8 derived or adapted here, 7 assumed. Each lists how it is checked: 22 automated verification checks (python -m dolle_sim verify, also part of the test suite), which you can also run in your browser on the Verify tab. Some components have no automated check, and say so.

**What is not certain is the material data, and it matters a lot.** With the commonly used values, carbon (graphite) is predicted to stick below 38 mm/s and silica below 6.2 mm/s. Of that 6.2x ratio, 2.5x comes from the published surface energies, 1.7x from the published elastic constants and 1.5x from our assumed restitution values. The assumed damping also sets most of each threshold: without it graphite would stick only below 16 mm/s. Published estimates for dry silica in vacuum are up to ten times the commonly used surface energy, which would make silica about as sticky as graphite. Measuring this is what DOLL-E is for.

**Origin key:** *published*: equation or value from the cited paper · *standard*: textbook physics or numerics · *derived*: derived or adapted for DOLL-E from standard physics · *assumed*: a modelling choice or value we chose.

## Contact physics (decides stick or bounce)

| component | based on | how it is checked | assumptions and limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| **JKR adhesive contact**<br>`contact.py: JKRContact, _resolve_core` | *published*: Elastic-adhesive contact force F(a) = 4E\*a^3/(3R) - sqrt(8 pi w E\* a^3) and overlap delta(a) = a^2/R - sqrt(2 pi w a/E\*). The contact snaps on at delta = 0 and breaks only at delta_c < 0. This adhesion hysteresis is why slow grains stick. The sticking THRESHOLD is set by the hysteresis plus the viscoelastic damping (next item), and for the default materials the damping dominates. Without damping the thresholds would be 15.6 mm/s (graphite) and 3.8 mm/s (silica) instead of 38 and 6.2 mm/s, so the JKR hysteresis supplies only ~17% (graphite) and ~38% (silica) of the threshold impact energy. Every impact is integrated in time with ~nanosecond steps; no outcome is read from a table.<br>*Johnson, Kendall & Roberts (1971) Proc. R. Soc. A 324, 301; Chokshi, Tielens & Hollenbach (1993) ApJ 407, 806* | With damping switched off, the integrated sticking speed equals the JKR hysteresis energy (3.825 vs 3.827 mm/s). The elastic limit returns e = 0.9999. The outcome depends only on the impact energy, to 1e-15.<br>`verify`: threshold, elastic, energy_scaling | JKR is the large-Tabor-parameter limit of continuum adhesion theory (accurate for mu above ~3-5). For 100 um grains (interaction range 0.2-0.4 nm) mu is ~7-14 for graphite (JKR applies) but only ~1-2 for silica and <1 for forsterite. That is the JKR-DMT transition, where JKR overstates the adhesion hysteresis, so the silica sticking speed is probably overestimated. Grains are smooth spheres; real grains are rough. |
| **Viscoelastic damping**<br>`contact.py: _resolve_core` | *derived*: Damping force -2 sqrt(5/6) beta sqrt(2 E\* a m\*) times the approach speed. This is the Tsuji et al. (1992) / Hertz-Mindlin form for non-adhesive Hertz contacts (as in EDEM and LIGGGHTS). We evaluate it with the JKR contact radius a, so it also acts at zero and negative overlap while the contact neck holds; that adaptation is ours. beta is set from each material's dry restitution e_dry.<br>*Tsuji, Tanaka & Ishida (1992) Powder Technol. 71, 239* | Automated: e = 0.8501 for e_dry = 0.85 (non-adhesive impact at 0.1 m/s). They set most of each threshold (see the JKR item). With equal e_dry (0.85) the predicted carbon/silica threshold ratio drops from 6.2x to 4.1x. Restitution is assumed independent of speed. |
| **Plastic yield**<br>`contact.py: _resolve_core` | *published*: Above the limiting contact pressure p_y, loading becomes linear in overlap. Unloading is elastic on the flattened contact (radius R_p, permanent indentation delta_p), so the pull-off force rises to 1.5 pi w R_p.<br>*Thornton & Ning (1998) Powder Technol. 99, 154* | Without adhesion or damping, restitution matches the Thornton-Ning closed form to 0.0001. Yield starts far above the sticking speeds (0.8 m/s for graphite, 11 m/s for silica), so the predicted thresholds are identical with yield switched off. The raised pull-off of a flattened contact is not tested. |
| **Dissimilar materials**<br>`materials.py: pair_props` | *standard*: Effective modulus 1/E\* = sum (1 - nu^2)/E. Work of adhesion w = 2 sqrt(gamma1 gamma2) (Berthelot-type mixing rule). Combined restitution sqrt(e1 e2). | Not checked: all contact checks use identical materials, where the mixing rules reduce to identities. In the shipped configs they are used for grain-wall contacts. | The mixing rules for w and e_dry are common approximations, not exact. |
| **Bounce impulse with friction**<br>`simulation.py: _impulse, _K_body` | *standard*: Rigid-body impact of two bodies, using the full 3x3 collision matrix at the contact point. The normal restitution comes from the contact model, inside a Coulomb friction cone. This is how spin and translation exchange energy.<br>*Stronge (2000) Impact Mechanics, Cambridge University Press* | 200 random oblique impacts on aggregates give the exact normal restitution (error 6e-16) and stay inside the friction cone. 2000 eccentric impacts never gain energy.<br>`verify`: impulse, friction_energy | Friction coefficient 0.3 and tangential restitution 0 are assumed global constants, not per-material. Rigid-body friction laws can create energy in eccentric impacts (a known problem). The code detects this and uses the frictionless impulse instead, counted in n_events['friction_energy_guard']. |
| **Sticking: rigid aggregates**<br>`simulation.py: _merge` | *assumed*: Grains that stick become one rigid body, conserving momentum and angular momentum. Aggregates keep spinning. | Momentum and angular momentum are conserved to 1e-16 through merging and splitting.<br>`verify`: conservation, energy_scaling | Only the normal impact speed decides stick or bounce; tangential speed and spin do not. Once stuck, grains cannot roll, slide or restructure. During an impact the whole aggregate (or a wall-bound grain together with the wall) is rigid, so the contact sees the rigid-body effective mass 1/(n.K.n). A grain hitting an aggregate therefore sticks at a lower speed than it would on a single grain (threshold ~ 1/sqrt(m_eff)), and on a wall-bound grain at ~1/sqrt(2) of the grain-grain threshold. |
| **Fragmentation thresholds**<br>`simulation.py: _fragment` | *published*: Erosion above 3 N_c E_break and shattering above 10 N_c E_break, where E_coll is the translational relative kinetic energy of the two bodies (normal plus tangential, no spin) and E_break is the aggregate's own grain-grain bond energy. Our choices: erosion removes only the struck grain, shattering breaks every aggregate involved into single grains.<br>*Dominik & Tielens (1997) ApJ 480, 647* | The thresholds themselves are not checked against an independent reference (only the energy and momentum bookkeeping, next item). | The 3x and 10x factors were derived for aggregates of sub-micron grains that can roll and restructure. Using them for 100 um rigid aggregates is an extrapolation. Because E_coll includes tangential motion, a fast grazing impact qualifies, but it breaks bonds only if it dissipates enough energy. |
| **Fragmentation energy budget**<br>`simulation.py: _fragment, _separate, _detach, _split_disconnected` | *derived*: The only energy available is what the bounce actually removed from the rigid bodies, E_diss = P.v_rel - P.K.P/2 (exact for any impact geometry). Each broken contact costs E_break. If E_diss cannot pay for a shatter, the struck grain is eroded instead; if it cannot pay for that either, nothing breaks. Fragments separate radially with the rest, solved so their kinetic energy rises by exactly that amount (momentum and angular momentum conserved). Grains left without a contact become separate bodies. | Every impact is measured just before and after it is resolved. The cases are grains hitting spinning chains and L-shaped aggregates at 0.02-3 m/s from random directions and offsets, spinning aggregates hitting a wall, and a closed box of 300 spinning graphite grains: ~1200 impacts, ~95 with fragmentation. Kinetic plus bond energy never increases (max 1e-13 of the impact energy), and no aggregate keeps grains that no longer touch. Momentum is conserved to 1e-16.<br>`verify`: fragmentation_energy, conservation | How a real impact splits its dissipated energy between heat and breaking contacts is unknown. Here all of it may go into breaking contacts. |
| **Wall contacts and coatings**<br>`simulation.py: _resolve_wall; materials.py` | *assumed*: The same JKR contact model, with the wall as a flat, infinitely massive body. A wall_adhesion_factor scales the wall adhesion to represent coatings and roughness. | Vibrating walls never let a grain out (~500 bounces, no penetration, no position corrections needed). The wall sticking probability is not tested.<br>`verify`: walls | The default anti-stick wall (fluorinated glass, gamma 0.012 J/m^2, adhesion factor 0.05) is a design assumption that must be measured on the real hardware. With factor 0.05 the grain-wall Tabor parameter is ~0.1-0.9 (DMT regime), so JKR is used outside its range there and probably overstates wall sticking. |
| **Vibrating walls**<br>`simulation.py: _sample_wall_phase` | *derived*: Wall speed at impact is drawn from the flux-weighted phase distribution. A grain is more likely to meet the wall while the wall moves toward it, in proportion to the relative speed. | Grains never leave the chamber, and the position safety net never had to act.<br>`verify`: walls | Only the wall's velocity is modelled, not its displacement. Each impact draws an independent phase. Not compared with a deterministic moving-wall model for grains slower than the wall speed. |
